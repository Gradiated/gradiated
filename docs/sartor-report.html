Sartor: can we host AI models far cheaper than Fireworks & Baseten?

Findings from a one-day research sprint · 11 July 2026 · input to a decision on whether to found a company on this thesis. Expandable sections carry the technical detail; the document is self-contained and can be pasted into an AI assistant for follow-up questions.

TL;DR: We tested whether a company that customises everything — the serving software, small modifications to the AI models themselves, and eventually its own chips — can host open-source AI models at a fraction of what today's providers charge. After building a working prototype engine, a calibrated cost simulator, and running it against five frontier models, the central estimate is ~4× cheaper versus today's best possible practice (with an 88–90% probability of at least 3×), and market prices sit another 2–4× above that. The strategy works in stages: software first (profitable from day one on rented GPUs), custom chips later once volume justifies them.

1. The idea, in plain terms

Companies like Fireworks and Baseten host open-source AI models (like China's GLM or Kimi) and charge per million tokens (a token ≈ ¾ of a word). Their software is deliberately general: it must run any model, for any customer, with fast response times. Generality has a cost — we call it the abstraction tax.

Our thesis: since AI now makes software extremely cheap to produce, you can afford to build a bespoke stack for each important model — custom serving code, custom low-level compute routines, small surgical changes to the model itself (then re-trained briefly to "heal"), and ultimately a chip designed for that model family. Each layer of customisation recovers cost that generality leaves on the table.

The target customers are background AI agents — coding assistants, automation, batch processing — which don't care about instant responses. That matters because tolerating a queue lets you run hardware flat-out (85%+ busy vs the 40–60% typical for interactive services), which is one of the biggest hidden cost factors in this business.

2. What we built today (so the numbers are grounded)

A real serving engine ("barge"), written from scratch in Rust, that actually runs real models on a real machine. Verified correct against independent implementations, then benchmarked.
A cost simulator ("sartor") that models the entire stack — chip, memory, compute routines, serving policy, model architecture, workload — and outputs cost per million tokens for any combination.
Real model surgery: we cut parts out of a real model's weights, re-trained it, and measured both quality and speed — closing the loop between prediction and reality.
Calibration discipline: the simulator is only trusted where it matches measurements — ours locally, plus published production numbers from DeepSeek and NVIDIA-class hardware benchmarks.
How the simulator works

A simulation run is a pure function over five specifications: the model's architecture (layers, attention type, mixture-of-experts structure), the hardware (compute speed, memory bandwidth and capacity, interconnect, price, power), the maturity of the compute kernels, the serving policy (batching, caching), and the workload (request sizes). It walks a single decoding step operation-by-operation — every matrix multiplication is costed as the worst of: launch overhead, arithmetic time at a shape-dependent efficiency, or the time to stream its weights from memory. On top sits a replay of a realistic serving loop (requests arriving, batching, memory filling up). It reproduces our local measurements within ~11% across three model sizes, predicted the speed effect of a real model surgery within 0.1%, and matches published datacentre benchmarks once a single explicit "reality factor" of 0.73 is applied (real systems achieve ~73% of an idealised model — we measured that gap rather than hiding it).

It was also wrong five times today — each error caught within hours by the calibration anchors. That's the point of the method: it self-corrects against reality.

3. The headline findings

3.1 Cost position: the layers of advantage

These are cost advantages versus the best practice incumbents could run — not savings to pass through. Incumbent prices sit 2–4× above best-practice cost; a cost advantage lets a new entrant choose any point between undercutting for share and holding price for margin (to fund capital expenditure).

Monte-Carlo verdict for software+surgery+chip vs best-practice cost: median ~3.9×, 90% chance of ≥3× (1,000 samples, individual optimisations allowed to fail); floor ~2.4× under maximally pessimistic surgery assumptions. Spot stacks on top for the rented phase.

What this means as a business (GLM-class output currently priced ≈ $2.00/Mtok): at our software+surgery cost (~$0.23 realised) we hold an 88% gross margin at incumbent prices, or 77% while undercutting them 2×; on spot (~$0.11) those become 94% / 89%. The cost advantage funds either market share or capex — that choice, not the technology, is the pricing strategy.

What "surgery" means and what was measured
  • Measured on real models today: compressing the model's short-term memory (the "KV cache") to 8-bit cost essentially zero quality (+0.06% on the standard quality metric). Cutting 33% of attention heads out of a real model and re-benchmarking it: our simulator predicted a 1.050× speedup; the measurement was 1.051×.
  • Strong precedent: one frontier lab (Moonshot/Kimi) already ships its flagship model in 4-bit weights — the industry accepts these techniques.
  • Still to prove: the more ambitious changes (sharing memory between layers, rerouting the model's internal "experts") need a proper re-training and evaluation campaign on a mid-size model (~$50k of compute) before we'd claim them.

Why the fixes differ per model

Tracing where every byte moves during generation showed three different bottlenecks: GLM-5.2 spends 77% of its memory traffic on just 2.3% of its weights (the parts every token must touch — so the fix is amortising them over bigger batches); Kimi-K2's cost is streaming its enormous "expert" weights (fix: compression and retrofitting features like multi-token prediction that DeepSeek already proved on the same architecture); MiniMax-M2 is strangled by its per-token memory footprint (fix: memory-sharing surgery, which roughly doubles how many requests fit on a chip). Same toolkit, three different prescriptions — and the best chip design came out different for each model too (different amounts of expensive HBM memory). This is the concrete form of the "abstraction tax" argument.

3.3 The custom-chip step is a milestone, not a gamble

Designing a chip costs $100–500M before the first unit ships. We computed the break-even: the chip beats staying on rented GPUs once you'd deploy roughly 3,000–14,500 chips — a fleet serving about a trillion tokens a day, i.e. once you've already become a large provider. So the sequencing is: software business first (profitable on rented GPUs, no capital outlay), chip when volume earns it. Meanwhile, the software phase automatically produces the data (per-model traffic maps) that is the chip's design spec.

3.4 We checked the radical alternative: putting the whole model in ultra-fast on-chip memory (the Cerebras approach)

Simulated on identical workloads: 76× more expensive per token than our approach for frontier-scale models (still 11× if their hardware were four times cheaper). The physics: their memory type (SRAM) costs 20–50× more per gigabyte, today's frontier models are enormous but only use a small fraction of themselves per token — the worst possible shape for that design. Their niche is speed-at-any-price; ours is cost. The industry's direction of travel (bigger, sparser models) widens this gap in our favour.

3.5 Robustness checks (overnight round)

  • Does the edge survive next-generation incumbent hardware? Against a tuned Blackwell-era (B200) incumbent, the co-design advantage is 3.8× (model surgery alone on their hardware: 1.75×). Compressed from the Hopper-era 6.5×, still well above the 3× viability bar.
  • Does it survive incumbent software improving? At 10–30%/yr incumbent improvement, the rented-phase edge decays from ~3.7× to 3.2–3.5× over three years; the durable floor (~2.9×) is the part incumbents structurally can't copy — per-model surgery and the batch-shaped fleet.
  • Fleet economics, computed not asserted: deferrable traffic alone is worth 1.78× (99% vs 56% hardware utilisation in a 14-day demand simulation); adding interruptible ("spot") capacity brings the fleet factor to 2.27× with deadlines met — capacity that latency-bound providers structurally cannot use.
  • Our own software keeps compounding: a second optimisation round on the prototype engine (fusing compute kernels) measured 2.9× further throughput, moving it from ~14% to ~31% of the best hand-tuned reference on the same hardware — evidence the custom-software layer is deep, not a one-off.
  • Business phases: at half of incumbent list prices, modelled gross margins run 77% (day-1, rented GPUs) → 86% (hybrid fleet) → 90% (owned) → 94% (custom chip); each capex phase is gated by a volume milestone, not a bet.

4. Structure of the opportunity

  • Target traffic: background/batch AI workloads (agents, pipelines) — a fast-growing token pool, latency-tolerant, and structurally suited to a high-utilisation fleet. Utilisation is one of the largest cost factors in this business, and queueable traffic makes high utilisation achievable by construction.
  • Cost position: layers 1+2 alone (no custom hardware) put modelled cost 2–3× below current market prices at sustainable margins.
  • Defensibility: reaching the co-designed cost point requires model weights + re-training capability + per-model analysis machinery + (later) silicon — a conjunction of capabilities. Each competitor class has one; almost none has all. And the target keeps moving with every model release, which favours whoever has automated the analysis; today's prototype is a first version of that automation.
  • Evidence standard: every number here is measured locally, anchored to published production data, or carried with explicit error bars, and is labelled as such.

5. What we're not sure about yet (honest list)

  • No measurements on datacentre GPUs yet — everything datacentre-scale is anchored to published benchmarks, not our own runs. (~$20k rental campaign fixes this.)
  • The bolder model surgeries need a re-train + quality evaluation at 30–100B scale (~$50k) before their savings can be claimed.
  • Chip cost estimates use industry-standard component pricing, not vendor quotes.
  • The business risk that matters most isn't technical: landing enough steady batch volume early so the fleet runs hot. Economics degrade badly at low utilisation.
  • Incumbent software (vLLM/SGLang) improves continuously — the software edge must be re-earned per model, which is why the product is the automated analysis machine, not a one-off optimisation.

Appendix: numbers & glossary (for digging / for Claude)

Key measured results from today
  • Our Rust engine vs vLLM (the industry-standard open-source server), identical hardware/model/workload: 2.1× faster (525 vs 246 tokens/sec output).
  • Simulator accuracy: +2.7% on the model it was tuned on; +10.0% and +10.2% on two models it had never seen (out-of-sample); prediction of a real surgery's speed effect: 1.050× predicted vs 1.051× measured.
  • Real-weights surgery: 8-bit KV cache lossless (+0.06% perplexity); global 4-bit KV harmful (+26%) — so per-layer selectivity is required; head-pruning destroys quality before healing (perplexity 16.6→82.5), demonstrating that the re-training step is where all the value lives.
  • Monte-Carlo verdict (1,000 samples, individual optimisations allowed to fail): co-design median 3.91× [10th pct 2.98, 90th pct 5.17]; P(≥3×)=0.90; the surgically-modified model needs a cheaper chip in 98% of samples.
  • Anchors: DeepSeek's published production throughput (H800 GPUs) and SGLang's published GB200 benchmark are both consistent with the simulator under one explicit realisation factor (0.73) and a fleet duty factor (~0.53).
Glossary (plain-English)
  • Token: the unit AI text is billed in; roughly ¾ of an English word. Prices are quoted in $ per million tokens ($/Mtok).
  • Inference / serving: running a trained model to answer requests (vs training it).
  • vLLM / SGLang: the leading open-source serving software incumbents build on.
  • KV cache: the model's per-conversation working memory; often the thing that limits how many requests fit on a chip.
  • Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): modern model design where only a small fraction of the model "activates" per token — huge total size, modest per-token compute.
  • Prefix caching: reusing computation when many requests share the same beginning (agents resend their context constantly — hit rates of 90%+ are achievable).
  • MTP (multi-token prediction): a trick letting the model emit ~1.7 tokens per step instead of 1.
  • HBM: the very fast (and expensive) memory stacked next to AI chips; usually the biggest hardware cost line.
  • Healing: brief re-training after modifying a model, to restore quality.
  • Realisation factor: our measured 0.73 ratio between idealised simulation and what tuned real systems achieve — applied to all absolute claims.
