# From sand to...

Here we will follow a path through all the layers of abstraction, from the atoms in a grain of sand to the thoughts generated by an LLM. As we pass each layer we will combine blocks to build upon the previous one, performing more and more complex tasks, looking at an example of each successive structure and what it can accomplish.

## Sand and metal

We start with quartz, a crystal of silicon dioxide, one silicon atom for every two of oxygen. In its pure form, it takes on a hexagonal lattice structure, producing a six-sided mineral. <rotating line-drawn quartz cluster> Heat it with carbon and you get silicon. Clean it up, melt it inside a quartz container, and grow one huge crystal. <the cluster melts and becomes a black Czochralski ingot> Then slice that crystal into thin wafers: the blank canvas for a chip. <one wafer is sliced off, turns edge-on and zooms into a 2D cross-section>

A silicon crystal can be sparsely seeded with electron-rich atoms (for example phosphorus) or electron-poor ones (boron). These alter its electrical properties. Here is a cross-section of our wafer, lightly seeded with boron while the crystal was grown. <cross-section, hollow circles for boron> Dopants can be implanted with what is essentially an atom gun, at the positions we choose. Here we have planted two islands of phosphorus-doped silicon in a sea of boron. <filled dots fire in from above and settle into two islands> We can now build our first transistor. Place three wires: two just touching the islands, and one in the middle, slightly separated from the silicon. <three wires come down from the top>

These wires are externally held at a voltage. Think of a voltage as an elevation map across fields of wires and circuits. Electrons, the conduits of information processing, like to flow downhill, from a peak at 1 volt to a valley at 0 volts. Let us set the left wire to 1 volt and the right wire to 0 volts. <1 V and 0 V appear over the outer wires> Electrons now want to flow from the left wire to the right one. But if the central wire, the gate, is also held at 0 volts, this setup has been carefully designed so that a built-in voltage hill [1] sits between the two islands, and the electrons cannot get over it. <electrons gather against the hill and cannot climb it> Raise the gate to 1 volt and the barrier is designed to lower. Electrons flow through this newly opened valley and go off to do useful things, perhaps turn on a light bulb. <the hill drops, electrons stream across, a bulb on the right lights up> This is the basic picture of an n-type MOSFET, one of the most common transistors used today: a switch with no moving parts.

It turns out that if you can make many voltage-controlled switches, you can combine them to do anything: any arithmetic, any function, any program and now, any ideating. We will follow this set of abilities as we expand in complexity.

## Logic

At their lowest level, computers only deal with basic logic. Logic is processed through entities known as gates, each made of a few transistors. A gate takes one or many inputs, a voltage at 0 or 1, flips and switches its transistors as this input signal passes through, and delivers 1s and 0s as output. The NOT gate is the most trivial example: a 1 in becomes a 0 out, and a 0 becomes a 1. <the cross-section becomes the transistor's circuit symbol, only the gate labelled> Zooming out from the transistor level, it is two transistors in a circuit diagram. The lower one is our n-type switch. The upper one, drawn with a small circle on its gate, is its mirror image: a p-type MOSFET, made with the dopants swapped, which turns on at 0 volts instead of 1. Whichever way A goes, exactly one of the pair conducts and pulls the output to the other rail, so any bit A that comes in leaves flipped as Ā. <NOT gate: p-type above, n-type below, between a 1 rail and a 0 rail, A in on the left, Ā out on the right>

Add two more transistors and we get a NAND gate: its output is 1 unless both A and B are 1. <NAND gate: four transistors, inputs A and B, out> Four transistors is already too much to draw every time, so from here on a NAND gate gets a symbol: a D with a small circle at its nose for the inversion. The two inputs go in on the flat side and the one output comes out of the circle. <the circuit shrinks and dims, the D draws itself around it, then the circle and the stubs appear> This is the most important building block: it can be shown that enough NAND gates, cleverly combined, can build any logical operation at all.

## Arithmetic

Let's zoom out further. Fit many of these NAND gates into a black box with three inputs and two outputs, and you get a block that adds the digits aᵢ and bᵢ, together with a carry-in cᵢ, into the output sᵢ. What happens if they are both 1? As in primary-school addition, we have to carry the 1: that is cᵢ₊₁. <full adder box with NAND tiles fading inside it; worked digits 10110 + 01101 with the current column in bold, carry-in 0, carry-out 1> Send that carry into the next identical block, which adds aᵢ₊₁, bᵢ₊₁ and cᵢ₊₁ to give the next digit sᵢ₊₁. Repeat n times and you can add any two n-bit numbers. <four blocks chained by carry arrows: 0011 + 0110 = 1001, carries 0 1 1 0 under the arrows, carry-in 0> Once you can add, subtraction and multiplication follow easily, and our toolset has grown.

We have recycled the four-transistor NAND over and over to build a several-hundred-transistor adder, and the counts are racking up. Every clock cycle, a fraction of the transistors flip and switch voltages, which takes energy: the heat dissipated into the surrounding silicon is proportional to C V² f. <P ∝ C V² f, caption: heating: capacitance × voltage² × clock> The capacitance, C, depends on the shape and layout of the wires and can be minimised during chip design. V can be lowered only at the cost of noise margin and switching speed, and lowering the clock speed f slows everything down directly. Heat is a budget we will keep encountering as the counts climb.

## Memory

Let's zoom back in for a moment and make one small change: we allow outputs to feed back into inputs. Here is a collection of NAND gates with two inputs, a data bit D and a write-enable WE, and an output Q. While WE is 1, Q copies D. Drop WE to 0 and Q holds whatever it last saw. A wire loops back from Q into the input of the NAND gate below it, and that one back into the gate producing Q. <four NAND symbols, D and WE in, Q out, the two feedback wires draw themselves> How does a loop create memory? Until now every circuit was a pure function of its inputs. This one has state: its output can depend on something calculated during a previous run, not only on the current inputs, so what it outputs now depends on what happened in the past.

## Programs

With that in our arsenal, we can zoom back out and make another forest of NANDs, this time packaged into a block called a look-up table, or LUT. Done correctly, you get a black box with three inputs and a single-bit output. Inside, eight of the memory bits we just built are programmed to map each of the eight possible inputs (000, 001 and so on) to a 0 or a 1. It is a truth table stored as an array, indexed by the input bits, and it gives any function of three bits. <LUT box: x₀ x₁ x₂ in, eight memory bits reading 0 1 1 0 1 0 0 1, y out> Zooming out, we can stack many of these arbitrary function maps side by side. The wiring between them is set by memory bits too, so the whole chip is configured by loading data, the way you would load a program. Together they implement any Boolean function. This is the basic description of an FPGA. <grid of LUTs wired together through programmable switches>
