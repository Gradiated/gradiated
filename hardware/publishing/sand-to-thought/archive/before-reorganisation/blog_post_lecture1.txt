# From sand to...

Here we will follow a worldline through all the layers of abstraction from the atoms in a grain of sand to the thoughts generated by an LLM. As we pierce each layer, we will combine blocks to build upon the previous layer to perform more and more complex tasks, and look at each superseding structure and what each layer can accomplish.

## Sand and metal

We start with quartzite, a rock full of silica. Heat it with carbon and you get silicon. Clean it up, melt it in a quartz container, and grow one huge crystal. Then slice that crystal into thin wafers: the blank canvas for a chip.
<video of rotating quartz crystal, drawn not real, as you scroll down this bit it melts and turns into a black blob that is then sliced up>

Each silicon crystal is sparsely populated with electron-rich (e.g. phosphorus) or electron-poor (e.g. boron) atoms <Zoom into a cross section of a wafer, which is doped with one color which is boron>. These alter the electrical properties and can be implanted with what is essentially an atom gun to the positions we chose <atoms fly in and bombard two regions>. Here we have placed two trenches of phosphorus-doped silicon in a sea of boron dopants. We can now build our first transistor: we place three wires to just touch these two trenches, and one in the middle - slightly separated from the silicon <add these three wires in from the top>. These wires are externally held at a voltage. Think of a voltage as an elevation map across fields of wires and electronic circuits. Electrons, which are the waterways of information processing, like to ``flow'' downhill, from a peak at 1 volt to a valley at 0 volts. Let us set the left wire to 1 volt and the right wire to 0 volts <add the numbers to picture>. In this case, electrons want to flow from the left wire to the right one. However, if the central wire - the gate - is also held at 0 volts, this setup has been specially designed so that there is a built-in voltage hill in between the two doped trenches <show electrons going into the left valley but just cannot get up the hill>, blocking the electrons from flowing. However, by raising the gate voltage to 1, the barrier is designed to lower, and electrons are now able to flow through this newly etched valley <have electrons flow through>. This is the basic picture of an n-type MOSFET, one of the most common transistors used today: it is essentially a switch with no moving parts, as these electrons are now able to go a and do useful things; perhaps turn on a light bulb. 

Now, almost by magic, it turns out that if you can make many voltage controlled switches, you can combine them in such a way to do anything - any arithmetic, any function, any program and now - any ideating. We will follow this set of abilities as we expand in complexity. 

## Logic

At their lowest level, computers only deal with basic logic. Logic is processed through entities known as ``gates'' - and are made up of a few transistors. Each gate takes one or many inputs (a voltage at 0 or 1), flips and switches all the transistors, and then delivers one or many 1s and 0s as an output. The NOT gate is the most trivial example: flip a 1 input to a 0, and a 0 to a 1. Zooming out from the transistor level, we can draw this out as a combination of two transistors in a circuit diagram: any bit input A that comes in will leave flipped as A-bar. Add two more transistors, and we get a NAND gate - this is the most important building block, as from this one gate it can be shown that one can build any logical operation, and with enough NAND gates cleverly combined, any function.

## Arithmetic 

Let's zoom out further. If you fit many of these NAND gates into a black box as shown, with three inputs and two outputs, <ai, bi, ci, is, ci+1 and with faded 1s and 0s next to a and b and s in columns> you get a block that can add together the digits of ai and bi to the output si. What happens if they are both 1? As in primary school addition, we have to carry the 1 - this is handled by ci+1, which we can then send into the next identical addition block which will in fact add together ai+1, bi+1 and ci+1 to give you the next output digit si+1. This can be repeated n times, to perform addition between any two n-bit numbers. If we can do addition, you can quite easily pull off subtraction and multiplication - and we have expanded our toolset. 

We have recycled the 4 transistor NAND gate structures over and over to build a several-hundred transistor addition block. As you can start to see, we are rapidly racking up the transistor counts. Every time an operation needs to be done, during each clock cycle, these transistors will have to flip and switch voltages, which takes energy. In fact, the heat leakage into the surrounding silicon by each transistor is proportional to C V^2 f. C, the capacitance, depends on the shape, structure and layout of the wires, and can ideally be minimized during chip design. The voltage V must be lowered at the risk of small-signal errors, and the clock speed f must also be kept low without significantly slowing computation.

## Memory

Let's zoom back in for a moment, and make one small change: we allow outputs to feed back into inputs. Here we have a collection of NAND gates with two inputs D and WE, and output Q. We have looped a wire back around from Q into the input of the NAND gate below it, and that back into the NAND gate producing Q. How does the introduction of a loop create memory? Because now the circuit's outputs are able to depend on something that was calculated at least during a previous run, not only the current inputs. What a circuit does must now depend on what happened in the past. 


## Programs 

With that in our arsenal, we can zoom back out and make another forest of NANDs: this time, we are going to package everything into a block called a look-up table (LUT). Done correctly, you get a black box with three inputs, and a single bit output. Within this black box, small memory circuits have been set up that can be programmed to map each of the eight possible inputs (000, 001 etc.) to an output of 0 or 1. This allows us to build any arbitrary 3-to-1 function. Zooming out, we can stack many of these arbitrary function maps side-by-side. By interconnecting them in a controlled way (with more memory bits) we can implement any Boolean function. This is the basic description of an FPGA.
