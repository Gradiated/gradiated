## The latest

Most avenues for making a language model smarter are linked to size. The most knowledgeable models are the largest ones, and among large models, those that have been trained on the largest set of quality data perform best. Looped models are interesting because they take an alternative path to increased performance.

By looping through certain sections of the model a few times, looped models can reason more without adding extra parameters. How to do this well is an active field of research, and until Friday, researchers only really had prototypes and very small models.

[Inkling 2 Mini](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) is a looped model, it's open-weight, and it's competent enough to be ranked by [independent benchmarks](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/inkling-2-mini). It rivals Qwen 3.8 27B at agentic coding, and it runs on a laptop. Here are the details.

## Inkling in context

Inkling is a model produced by Thinking Machines. Founded in early 2025, Thinking Machines is a San Francisco-based lab that is not building AGI. They outline their belief that AI is best used as a tool to extend human will and judgement in their thoughtful blog ["The Future Worth Building Is Human"](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/the-future-worth-building-is-human/).

The team believes human values don't average out, and that an ecosystem of AIs with great diversity is better than a handful of centralized all-purpose models. To build such a world, there would need to be strong open models, and useful tools to fine-tune them. They're working to produce both of the above.

Their first product, Tinker, launched in October 2025. Tinker is an API for fine-tuning open-weight models. Earlier this year they released their first model, Inkling. They trained Inkling from scratch and since its release in July they've also released Inkling-Small, Inkling 2 Small, and the focus of this announcement, Inkling 2 Mini.

The pitch emphasizes that Inkling 2 Mini is uniquely capable for its size, and is a base for further customization. It's a meaningful contribution to the research community, which can now experiment with a model that has a looped architecture.

## Inkling in action

The demo in the official announcement is fun and impressive. They run the model on a MacBook Pro in airplane mode up on an actual flight, and ask it to build a simple flight simulator. It does! You can even play it on the [announcement page](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/).

While it's nice to see the _what can be done on a laptop_ frontier move forward, Inkling is more of an offering to the research community than it is to product developers. Thinking Machines invites researchers to use the model to study the monitorability of looping models.

> We are particularly excited for researchers to investigate questions of monitorability, and to develop interpretability techniques for understanding the computation within loops.
>
> [Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/#monitorability-of-looped-transformers)

The idea is that for models that don't loop, they must write out text to reason with themselves. Model developers and even users can read that text, and learn how the model thought about the problem and what its approach was. This is formally referred to as the model's chain of thought.

Looping models can instead do more of their reasoning inside the loops, rather than writing out their full chain of thought. The concern is that if we can't read the full chain of thought, we may miss key details on how the model thinks. Did it understand the task? Is it confused or being devious?

<LoopsFigure />

This is the first open-weight model with strong enough capabilities to test how looping affects monitorability. Inkling 2 Mini will be put to work to help answer these questions. We do not know the architecture of large, proprietary frontier models, but rumours suggest this is an important avenue of research. Understanding how monitorability works on a small looped model is a sound starting place.

## Technically of note

#### Only the middle layers of the model loop

Inkling 2 Mini is a model composed of 24 layers. It's easy to imagine the model itself as a complex abstract thing and not as a very large math function. But it is a very large math function. It is mysterious how intelligence arises from simple parts repeated at great scale, but we must force ourselves to remember that large language models are themselves large math functions that take a vector input (the embedding for one token) and yield a vector output (which token, of the whole model vocabulary is a good next output).

With this in mind, we may discuss the layers. The model is a composite function, meaning it's made of a chain of smaller functions where the output of the first is a valid input for the next. Each layer takes a vector of the same size (for Inkling 2 Mini, a vector length 2,304), and outputs a vector with that size. The layers can be grouped into blocks based on repeating patterns. For Inkling 2 Mini the 24 layers are grouped into four blocks of six layers. That each layer accepts and outputs a vector of the same shape is what makes looping possible.

So how do the loops happen? Only blocks two and three are involved in the loop. A token passes through the first block once, and then moves through blocks two and three sequentially, three separate times before moving through the fourth block once. The idea here is that the middle blocks are where abstract reasoning likely happens. It's very hard to know what reasoning happens where, as a consequence of it being hard to understand how this math function can reason at all.

The intuition that the middle layers are the layers that are beneficial to loop through is based on an understanding that the model must do some translating between tokens and its own internal representations. That translating likely happens in the layers closest to the input and output. It is an intuition, and the Thinking Machines team did test different loop configurations. They found that looping through only the middle layers (blocks two and three) beat looping through the later layers (blocks three and four) on 27 of the 28 tests they ran.

The overall result here is a model that computes through 48 layers but only needs to store 24.

#### A research model with adjustable loop depth

Thinking Machines also published a research model called [TML-d24-varloop-research](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/TML-d24-varloop-research). The research model lets you set the loop depth anywhere between one and five at the time of inference, and the model card includes a bunch of benchmarks run at each loop depth.

It's quite cool to see that for tasks that require reasoning, adding more loops actually does improve the model's performance. On knowledge-based tasks, like closed-book factual recall benchmarks, looping has little to no improvement. This is obviously expected, but satisfying to see empirically.

What's particularly noticeable is the improvement looping makes on the model's ability to _find_ information. [BrowseComp](https://openai.com/index/browsecomp/) is a benchmark from OpenAI that tests an agent's ability to locate hard-to-find information on the web. Using search and tools effectively is a core agent task, and looping appears to have a big effect.

<LoopsChart />

The research model shared its pre-training with Inkling 2 Mini, and the two diverged in mid-training where the research model received training at every loop depth one through five. Most importantly, in post-training, the research model only underwent the reinforcement learning stage at loop depth three.

This does mean that the research model can't be used to truly compare loop depths fairly. It can merely suggest what is likely to happen as loop depths increase, which in itself is a tremendous step forward for research tooling.

## How to run it

This is not a hard model to run. If you have a laptop with more than 32 GB of RAM, you can run it locally. [Ollama](https://ollama.com/library/inkling-2-mini) handles the details, and even configures its use in a number of coding agents (Claude Code, OpenCode, etc.)
