## Feedback at Gradiated

We care a lot about sharing and receiving feedback openly. People can only work with real autonomy if information flows freely, and feedback is a big part of that.

That includes feedback to each other, so we know we're working on the right things in the right way. Telling a teammate something can feel awkward, especially if it isn't positive, or if they have more experience than you. But it's part of not letting each other fail.

Open and honest doesn't mean unkind. We expect feedback to be direct, and given with good intentions, to genuinely help the person and Gradiated get better. Keep it constructive and based on what you've observed rather than how you feel, and share an example where you can, so the other person understands the context.

## 360-degree feedback sessions

Every few months, we run a session where everyone gives and receives feedback with everyone else. It works best in person. We go round one person at a time, and each teammate shares one thing that person should keep doing and one thing they should change.

### Ground rules

- Everybody takes part. Think it through and write your notes beforehand, rather than winging it on the day.
- Read the two sections below, on giving and receiving feedback well, before you come.
- The balance of positive and constructive will vary, but spend more time on the constructive, even if you have a long list of positives. It's a chance to help each other grow.
- Give feedback to everyone, even people you don't work with directly. A sentence or two is fine.
- Don't pile on or repeat what someone else has said, unless you see it differently or can add context. Saying "+1" to the odd point you agree with is enough, so we don't spend minutes on feedback already made.
- You're responsible for writing down your own feedback and acting on it. Nobody will do it for you.
- What's said is for the people in the room, and doesn't need to be shared with anyone who wasn't there. You're welcome to follow up with anyone afterwards on feedback you gave or received.
- Use a notebook, or your phone at a push. A laptop makes it much harder to be present, so leave it closed.

## How to give good feedback

Giving feedback can be hard, so here are a few tips:

- If something went wrong, focus on what happened, not whose fault it was. Blame doesn't get anyone anywhere.
- Be as specific as you can, and give an example for context.
- If you might be missing context, a question can work better: "I've noticed you sometimes do X. Can you talk me through your thinking when you do that?"
- If it's about behavior, talk about the behavior and its effect on you, not the person's character: "When you do X, it makes me feel Y. Would you be willing to do Z instead?"
- Don't forget positive feedback. It's how people know what to keep doing.

We expect everyone to support each other by giving plenty of feedback. If you have something constructive to share, staying quiet isn't OK.

## How to receive feedback well

When someone makes the effort to give you feedback, return the favor by receiving it well. The better you are at hearing it, the more of it you'll get, and the more you'll grow.

A few things that help:

- Assume they mean well.
- Try not to hear an attack. Listen for what's behind the words.
- Play it back in your own words, or ask questions, to check you've understood.
- You don't have to accept all of it. It's usually worth taking time to reflect rather than reacting in the moment. Acknowledging feedback isn't the same as agreeing with it.
